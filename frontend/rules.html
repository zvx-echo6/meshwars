The basics

Every player is on one of seven teams: RED, GREEN, BLUE, PURPLE, YELLOW, ORANGE or PINK. You pick one when you join and it does not change.

The map is cut into fixed squares roughly 300 metres on a side. You claim a square for your team by being inside it and reaching the mesh from it — not by being there, and not by seeing it on a map. The radio has to actually get out.

A season runs six months. The team holding the most ground when it ends wins. Inside the season, each calendar month has its own winner, so there is always something close to play for.

Claiming a square

Everything starts with a ping: your position, plus which mesh nodes that transmission reached. Each node it reached is worth a tenth of a point, and a single ping can never be worth more than one point no matter how many it reached.

Per node reached0.1 points
Most one ping can earn1.0 points
A ping that reached nobodynothing at all

That last line is the rule people trip over. Being somewhere is not enough. A ping from a valley with no coverage is recorded, costs you nothing, and claims nothing.

Which nodes count differs by protocol and it is inverted between them — see MeshCore and Meshtastic.

Taking and holding

Points accumulate per team, per square. Ownership follows from them.

  • Unclaimed ground goes to the first team to paint it.
  • A square that just changed hands cannot be taken for fifteen minutes, whatever anyone scores. A capture you have to defend from the second it lands is not a capture.
  • After that it falls to whoever out-scores the team currently holding it. Only the holder's score matters — not the highest score among the other six. With seven teams there is no single rival, so each attacker is measured against whoever actually has the ground.
  • The first time a new person paints a square for their team it is worth an extra half point. Three people who each visit once beat one person who visits three times.
  • The same person cannot repaint the same square inside five minutes. Parking in one spot and transmitting does not run up a score.
  • Scores fade by a quarter point a day, toward zero. This is why a six-month season does not calcify: ground painted once and never revisited is worth nothing within weeks, so what you hold always means what you have reached recently.

Seasons

A season is 180 days. The two boards run their own seasons on their own clocks and started on different days, so they do not end together. The MeshCore season's end date is shown on the about page, read live rather than repeated here where it would go stale.

A team's standing is the squares it holds, plus the check-in points its players have earned, plus the points they have earned at Places Worth Going. All three count, and they count in the same units: one square held is worth the same as one check-in point.

When a season ends, the board resets and check-in points reset with it. Streaks do not — see streaks.

The weekly net

There is a net every Wednesday evening, 17:00 to 23:59 Boise time. Checking in during that window earns your team points on top of any ground you hold.

WhenWednesdays, 17:00–23:59 America/Boise
Base value25 points
How oftenonce per net, per player
On MeshCorepost in the #weekly-net channel
On Meshtasticsend a message containing #freq51

Posting five times earns once, the same as everyone else. A message before 17:00 or after midnight does not count — the window is the window.

This is a genuine way to play rather than a consolation prize. Points land in the same team total as squares, so a night on the net contributes whether or not you ever leave the house.

Streaks

Turning up every week is worth more than turning up once. The bonus starts on your second consecutive net and stops growing after the sixth.

Miss a net and you start again at 25.

A week where the net does not happen at all costs you nothing. A Wednesday only counts as a net if somebody checked in. If everyone skips a holiday week, nobody's run breaks.

Your streak survives a season boundary. The points it earned reset with everything else, but the run itself is a record of showing up, not territory. Streaks are counted per board — a MeshCore run and a Meshtastic run are separate.

Places Worth Going

Some squares aren't just squares. A summit, a park or a landmark inside one makes that square worth more than the ground around it. None of it is invented for the game: summits come from Summits On The Air, parks from Parks On The Air and the national protected-areas database PAD-US, and landmarks from OpenStreetMap.

Scored by effort, not by category

What a place is worth depends on how far it made you go, not what kind of place it is. Anywhere inside a town's own limits — summit, park or landmark alike — is worth five points, the same nickel whether you drove up a peak or walked to the courthouse. Step outside a town and the category starts to matter.

Inside a townOutside a town
Landmark510
Park525
Summit550 to 100, by height

A summit outside a town is the one value that is not flat. It slides with elevation: 50 points at 6,000 feet and below, climbing evenly to 100 points at 9,000 feet and above — a peak at 7,500 feet is worth 75. Being inside a town still overrules it outright. A summit you can park at in the middle of town is worth five points however tall it is.

That is on purpose. A summit you can park a car next to and a hot spring at the end of an hour of dirt road are both "a summit" and "a landmark" by category, but they are not the same errand. Scoring by effort instead of by category means the game rewards the drive, not the label, while a town's own places are worth showing up for but not worth grinding.

The weekly cap

Per placeone credit, per person, per week
Weekly ceiling100 points per person, whatever the mix
To scorea scoring ping, same as claiming a square — being there is not enough
Aircraftexcluded on MeshCore — see fair play

The ceiling is a budget, not a switch. Every place you reach spends its full value out of the hundred, and a place only pays out if its whole value still fits in what is left of the week — there is no partial credit. So a 100-point peak spends the week in a single trip, but two 75-point peaks do not: the first leaves 25 behind, and the second is turned away whole rather than paying you 25 for it. Nothing is lost by it — the place is simply still there next week.

Ten remote landmarks, four remote parks or one 9,000-foot summit all reach the ceiling. Anything lower leaves room for something else alongside it, which is the point: a big peak is a week's work, a lower one is an afternoon with room left over.

Points go to your own Explorer Score and to your team's total, the same shape check-in points already have.

Explorer is not a monthly honor. It is the name of an activity and of a ranking — the Explorer tab under Top Operators, alongside Wardrivers and NetOps. Your Explorer Score runs the length of the season and counts toward your total score, so it is never reset or scored month by month the way the honors below are. The monthly honors built on places count visits instead of points: Tourist, Park Hopper and Peak Tagger.

Which square scores

A landmark or a summit scores the single square its point falls in. A park with a mapped boundary at least the size of a grid square scores every square more than half inside that boundary — skirt its edge and only the squares mostly inside it count. A park smaller than a square, or without a boundary on file at all, scores the square containing its point instead, the same as a landmark. About one park in twenty has no boundary data on file; those still score, just by that simpler rule.

One square can carry more than one place — a landmark standing inside a large park, say. Only the most valuable one scores. Places do not stack: the lesser place is dropped rather than paid alongside the better one, because one trip to one square is one errand and it pays once. If that most valuable place is worth more than what is left of your week, or you already collected it this week, the square pays nothing rather than handing you the cheaper one instead — both are still there next week. Two places of exactly equal value on one square always resolve to the same winner, every week and for every player.

What makes the list

The list is deliberately narrow, and three things you might expect on it are not. Trailheads are left off because a trailhead is where you start going somewhere, not somewhere to go. Fire lookouts are left off because the peak a lookout stands on is already the summit you get credit for, and scoring both would pay you twice for one walk — for the same reason, a landmark sitting within about a hundred metres of a scoring summit is dropped in favour of the summit.

Summits have to have a real name. Roughly a fifth of the source list records unnamed peaks by their height instead of a name, or by a placeholder like "Peak 8", and those are left out — unless the number is 13,000 feet or more, where a peak genuinely is known by its elevation.

The weekly rotation

Summits and every park with a real, mapped boundary are always live. Landmarks, and the small parks whose mapped boundary doesn't fill a whole square, rotate instead: only some of them are live in any given week, chosen the same way for every player, with a minimum spacing between live picks so a dense downtown can't be farmed by visiting one block. A park with no boundary on file isn't treated as small for rotation, even though it scores like one — the game doesn't actually know its size, so it stays always active instead of guessing, the same as a summit.

The rotation turns over on the same Wednesday reset as the net and everything else — one clock for the whole game. A landmark that's live this week may not be next week, and that's deliberate: the point is to keep sending you somewhere new, not to hand out a fixed list once and let it go stale.

Monthly results

Each calendar month, Boise time, gets its own result on the results page.

A month is judged when it ends, not as it goes. Nothing is shown for the month in progress except when it closes. An award you can watch changing hands daily is not an award.

A month is scored on the month. Ground taken and points earned between its own dates — never a snapshot of the season standings. If it were a snapshot, whoever led in the first month would lead in all of them and none of it would mean anything. Falling behind early never puts the next month out of reach.

Ties are refused rather than split. If two people share the lead for an award, nobody gets it that month.

Registering a radio

Nothing you transmit earns anything until the radio it came from is registered to you. Every ping and every check-in is credited through that link.

On MeshCore, your radio is identified by its contact key — the first eight characters of its public key. MeshMapper binds it for you the first time you wardrive, provided its "Include Contact Key" setting is on. That setting being off is the single most common reason a player sees nothing happen. You can also pick your node from a list on the join page, or type the eight characters in yourself.

On Meshtastic, your radio is identified by its node ID, and you add it yourself on the join page. It cannot bind itself, because the packets MeshWars reads carry nothing that points back to you.

A radio belongs to one player. If a contact already registered to somebody else turns up under your key, the ping is dropped rather than credited — to either of you.

Your API key is shown once and only a hash of it is stored, so it cannot be recovered. A MeshCore player can read theirs back out of MeshMapper's own settings; otherwise an operator can issue you an extra key without breaking the one you have.

Fair play

A few mechanics exist to keep the game about reaching the mesh. All of them are narrow on purpose.

How precise your position has to be

A Meshtastic radio can be set to report its position roughly rather than exactly, and some are shipped that way. A rough position can put you anywhere inside a box a kilometre or more across, which is several squares wide — so a position that is not precise enough to say which square you were in claims nothing at all.

Squares are about 300 metres, so a position has to be good to roughly 180 metres or better to count. In the Meshtastic app that is the channel's position precision setting: leave it at full precision, or at least at the step that says within 182 metres. A packet that does not say how precise it is gets the same answer, because there is nothing to check — and the packets that omit it are the ones carrying no usable position in the first place.

This is a Meshtastic setting and it has no MeshCore equivalent. If you register a radio, drive somewhere with coverage, and nothing appears on the map, this is the first thing to look at.

Aircraft and impossible jumps

A radio at altitude reaches an absurd number of nodes, which would make the exploration awards meaningless. MeshWars works out your speed between consecutive positions — and the two boards act on that number differently, because they can afford to.

On MeshCore, faster than roughly 100 mph is marked as airborne. Those squares still count as territory — the radio genuinely reached those nodes, and that is what territory measures. What they do not count toward is Places Worth Going, and the awards built on reach and effort: Tourist, Park Hopper, Peak Tagger and Frontier. Nothing is taken away and nobody is penalised. Above roughly 900 mph the jump is read as a bad GPS fix rather than a flight, so a phone that briefly locates you in the next county does not disqualify a legitimate remote claim.

On Meshtastic, there is no airborne marking at all. A position that would have needed more than roughly 200 mph since your last one is thrown out instead, and claims nothing. The bar is deliberately far above MeshCore's: refusing a position costs you a square where labelling one does not, and two positions a few seconds apart in neighbouring squares can read as a surprisingly high speed from the grid alone. A refused position is also not used as the starting point for the next check, so one bad fix cannot make your next honest ping look like the impossible one.

Nothing catches a hovering aircraft; that is a known gap.

Automated check-ins

Quick Fingers is an award for reacting quickly to an event that happens on a schedule, which is an open invitation to a cron job. So it ships with its own defence.

A person posting from a phone lands somewhere different each week — scattered across minutes. A script fires at the same offset every time, to within a second or two. MeshWars looks at how much a player's check-in timing varies across the month, and when it barely varies at all over enough nets, that player is quietly skipped for Quick Fingers.

That is the whole extent of it. It is not a penalty, it is not an accusation, and it affects nothing else — the check-in still earns its points, the streak still counts, every other award is unaffected. A false positive costs somebody one novelty award, whereas punishing anyone on the strength of a statistical hunch would mean settling arguments that cannot actually be settled.

Sharing keys

A contact key bound to one player cannot earn for another. If a ping arrives under one player's API key carrying a radio registered to somebody else, it is dropped and logged rather than credited to either of them.

MeshCore and Meshtastic

Two boards, one game. Same seven teams, same grid, same scoring table, same rules on this page. What differs is how your position reaches MeshWars — and it is inverted between them — plus two checks on the ping itself, at the bottom of the table.

The inversion is not a design choice, it is what each network lets anyone observe. MeshCore tells you what your radio reached; Meshtastic tells you who logged your radio.

Everything else — teams, squares, capture, decay, seasons, the net, streaks, the honors — is identical. The two rows at the bottom of that table are the only places the boards judge a ping differently, and both are explained under fair play.
